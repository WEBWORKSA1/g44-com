Medication-overuse headache, often called rebound headache, happens when the medicines you take for headaches start causing more of them. The World Health Organization calls it the most common secondary headache, affecting up to 5% of some populations. The latest Global Burden of Disease analysis found that medication overuse accounts for more than a fifth of all health loss from headache disorders. The good news: it is largely preventable and reversible.

## The limits that matter

The international classification (ICHD-3) defines it as headache on **15 or more days a month** in someone who already has a headache disorder, with regular overuse for **more than 3 months** of:

| Medicine type | Overuse threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Simple pain relievers: paracetamol (acetaminophen), ibuprofen, aspirin, naproxen | 15 or more days a month |
| Triptans, ergots, opioids, combination pain relievers (for example with caffeine or codeine) | 10 or more days a month |
| A mix of types, none over its own limit | 10 or more days a month in total |

What counts is the number of **days** you take something, not the number of pills. Our [rebound-risk check](tools/rebound-risk.html) does the counting for you.

People who take pain relievers for other reasons, such as arthritis, without a headache disorder, do not usually develop it.

## Signs it may be happening

- Headaches have become more frequent over months.
- You wake with a headache, or it returns as each dose wears off.
- You take something "just in case".
- Preventive treatments seem to stop working.

## How it is treated

1. **Reduce or stop the overused medicine.** For simple pain relievers and triptans this can often be done straight away. Opioids, barbiturate-containing medicines and some sleeping tablets must be tapered under medical supervision. Never stop these suddenly on your own.
2. **Expect a rough patch.** Headaches often get worse for a week or two before improving.
3. **Start prevention.** A doctor can begin a preventive medicine at the same time, so the underlying migraine or tension-type headache is controlled.
4. **Set a rule for the future**, such as no more than two or three treatment days a week, and track it in a [diary](tools/headache-diary.html).

Most people who complete withdrawal see a large drop in headache days. Relapse is common in the first year, which is why counting days matters.
